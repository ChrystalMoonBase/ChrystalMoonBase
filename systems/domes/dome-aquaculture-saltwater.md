# Saltwater Aquaculture Dome
## Chrystal Moon Base — Food Production and Ecosystem

**Status:** Concept proposal  
**Author:** Berechja Kerkdijk  
**Licence:** CC0

## Overview

Two saltwater aquaculture domes are proposed for the Chrystal Moon Base. Each contains a managed saltwater ecosystem: fish for protein production, algae pillars for atmosphere processing and nutrition, and the biological infrastructure to keep both alive and productive.

Two domes rather than one provides redundancy — if one dome has a disease outbreak or system failure, the other continues producing. It also allows different species to be maintained in different conditions, increasing dietary variety.

---

## Why Saltwater Fish

Saltwater fish species available for aquaculture — sea bass, sea bream, turbot, tilapia (technically freshwater but salt-tolerant), and others — provide high-quality complete protein with a different nutritional profile than freshwater species. Dietary diversity is medically important for permanent residents. It is also psychologically important — monotonous food is a significant quality-of-life problem in isolated environments.

**Salt supply (honest caveat — corrected):** earlier text claimed sodium chloride could be extracted locally. This is unsupported: chlorine is scarce on the Moon (highland regolith holds only tens of ppm Cl), so making 3.5%-salinity seawater for whole domes is NOT sustainable from local resources. Salt should be treated as an IMPORT item, or saltwater aquaculture deferred to a later/optional phase. Sodium is locally available (as Na₂O in feldspar) but the chloride is the limiting element.

## The Algae Pillars

Transparent glass tubes — produced by M-Glass — filled with saltwater algae culture and illuminated from below or internally. Algae species suited to saltwater environments include:

- **Chlorella** — high protein, fast growing, excellent nutritional profile
- **Spirulina** (technically freshwater but adaptable) — 60–70% protein by dry mass, the most nutritionally dense food organism known
- **Nannochloropsis** — marine species, high in omega-3 fatty acids

The pillars serve simultaneously as:
- **Food producers** — algae harvested continuously, processed into food supplement
- **Atmosphere processors** — algae consume CO₂ and produce O₂, contributing to the dome's atmosphere management
- **Visual elements** — glowing blue-green columns of living material, creating a visually extraordinary environment

Pillar diameter: 20–40 cm. Height: 2–4 metres. Density: as many as structurally practical within the dome floor area.

## The Ecosystem Loop

**Nothing is wasted.** The saltwater aquaculture dome operates as a closed biological loop:

- Fish consume feed (algae + produced food pellets)
- Fish produce waste (ammonia, CO₂, faeces)
- Bacterial biofilters convert ammonia to nitrates
- Nitrates feed the algae
- Algae consume CO₂ and produce O₂
- Algae are harvested and fed back to fish and to human food supply
- Water is recirculated — losses from evaporation are captured by the dome atmosphere system and returned

The only inputs are energy (light and heat) and makeup water for evaporation losses. The only outputs are harvested fish, harvested algae, and excess O₂ to the base atmosphere. No waste leaves the dome that cannot be used somewhere else in the base.

## Dome Dimensions

Estimated diameter: 25–35 metres. The dome must be large enough to contain:
- Fish tank volume sufficient for meaningful protein production (TBD by aquaculture engineering)
- Sufficient algae pillar area for meaningful O₂ production
- Maintenance access for robots and human aquaculture staff
- Water treatment and filtration infrastructure (connected underground)

---

## Water Source

Saltwater is produced by dissolving imported salt in base-produced water (see salt caveat above — local chlorine is too scarce to supply this). Salinity is maintained at ~3.5% (Earth ocean equivalent). Water is recirculated with minimal loss; the modest salt losses are replenished from the imported reserve.

## Structural Notes

The dome uses the standard five-layer construction. For aquaculture domes, the inner glass layer must withstand the humidity and chemical environment of a saltwater ecosystem — the anti-decay coating is particularly important here, as salt water is corrosive to many materials over time.

All water connections (supply, return, waste) pass through the floor via the underground network. The floor itself is sintered regolith — impermeable, chemically resistant to saltwater, and easy to clean.
