# Growth Model — Gates, Not a Timeline

**Status:** Proposal and open questions — deliberately tentative. Nothing here is a
promise or a prediction. Each phase is a *gate*: it must prove itself before the next
one has any meaning.
**Licence:** CC0

---

## Why gates instead of a schedule

CMB is not planned as a fixed timeline with dates. It is planned as a **sequence of
gates**, where each stage has to actually work before the next stage is worth
starting. This keeps the project honest: if a gate cannot be passed, the project
stops there — without harm — rather than pushing forward on hope. *Pride comes
before a fall;* the gate structure is the practical answer to that.

A second principle runs alongside the gates: **a rolling build front.** Nothing waits
for a whole phase to be "finished." The moment a small usable area exists, it can be
used. A few square metres of sintered floor can already host the first small factory;
a sealed cavity can already become storage. Building and using happen continuously,
side by side, while the building goes on.

---

## The gates (each must work before the next begins)

1. **The prototype works** (`CMB8LF-S1`, 1:3.5 scale, on Earth). This is where
   everything starts. If this gate cannot be passed, the project does not proceed
   beyond commercial 3D-printing — and that is an acceptable, complete outcome.
2. **The full-size robot is built and works** (`CMB8LF-L1`), funded by the revenue
   that a working prototype makes possible.
3. **A team of these robots works autonomously together** — they dig, sinter, and
   build without human intervention. Only once this is proven does building a Pod
   make sense.
4. **The Pod is built and fully tested on Earth.** Only after that is the question of
   *how to get it to the Moon* even opened.
5. **The Pod lands and works on the Moon.** Only then does the rest of the picture
   begin. This is the point of no return, and it is deliberately placed last — after
   four proven gates — so that nothing irreversible happens until everything before
   it has already been demonstrated.

Each gate is also a **safe stopping point**: the prototype is a product, the
customers are revenue, the robots are a business. At no point is everything staked at
once. The project climbs a staircase where every step is a solid floor, not a ladder
where falling hurts.

## How scaling actually works (tentative)

The honest baseline is **sequential and proof-driven**: one Pod, proven autonomy,
then the next — not several launches at once. Under this baseline, the first Pod
carries a single power system, and capability grows Pod by Pod. The heaviest,
most energy-hungry factories (high-purity refining) therefore come late, once enough
power is in place.

A faster, fully-funded path (many Pods at once) is *not* assumed. It is recorded only
as a possibility, not a plan. A working first Pod would lower the project's biggest
risk — "does it work at all?" — from a claim to a demonstration, which could change
what becomes possible afterward. But the project is designed to stand on the
sequential, self-carried path regardless. The optimism is kept separate from the
planning on purpose.

## Open questions

1. What is the minimum demonstration at each gate that justifies opening the next?
2. How much of the build front can run continuously versus needing a new Pod's
   capability?
3. At which gate does autonomy need to be "good enough," and how is that judged?

## A personal note on expectations (from the founder)

CMB is developed in full honesty, including about its limits. There is a clear,
stepped route with deliberately minimal expectations behind it. The project is set up
so that, if those minimal expectations turn out not to be reachable, it can stop at
that gate without damage — the commercial activities (3D-printing and robotics) stand
on their own and carry it, and form the safety net. CMB is an investigation, not a
promise; it advances one proven step at a time, and whatever proves unreachable will
be named honestly as such. The specific internal thresholds remain the founder's own;
what is shared here is the attitude, not the numbers.
