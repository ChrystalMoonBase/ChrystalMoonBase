> *Simple. Immediate. Rigid. The fastest path to a stable mast — at the cost of working area and mass.*

---

## Philosophy

v1 prioritises speed and mechanical simplicity above all else. A single heavy penetrator drives deep into the regolith on touchdown. Flukes deploy. The mast is stable within hours. No waiting for sintering. No long vulnerability window.

The trade-off is mass and working area. v1 is the heaviest variant and provides the smallest footprint for robot operations.

---

## Key Specifications

| Parameter | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Wet mass | ~11,700–11,800 kg | Heaviest variant |
| Footprint diameter | 8–12 m | After fluke deployment |
| Penetrator mass | 380–420 kg | Titanium alloy |
| Penetration depth | 1.2–1.8 m | At 1.5–2.5 m/s touchdown velocity |
| Time to basic stability | Minutes to hours | Fluke lock |
| Time to permanent anchor | Weeks | Optional laser plug sintering |
| Sintering volume | Small optional plugs | Not a full ring |
| Terrain tolerance | Highest | Works on slopes up to ~5–8° |
| Mechanical complexity | Low | Fewest moving parts after touchdown |

---

## How It Works

The pod descends at 1.5 to 2.5 metres per second at engine cutoff. A 380 to 420 kilogram titanium alloy penetrator nose drives 1.2 to 1.8 metres into the regolith on impact. Spring-loaded barbed flukes deploy sideways in the subsurface, locking the structure laterally within minutes. Three electromechanical jacks level the pod to within 2 to 3 degrees.

In the weeks after landing, the robots optionally sinter short plugs around the penetrator base for additional long-term security.

---

## What Makes It Credible

Penetrator anchor concepts have been studied for lunar and planetary applications since the 1960s. The technology is mechanically simple: concentrated load, deep bite, immediate rigidity. There are no large deployment mechanisms to fail after touchdown. This approach has the fewest steps between landing and a stable, operational mast.

---

## What Makes It Difficult

Lunar regolith is not homogeneous. The upper 0.3 to 0.5 metres may be a loose unconsolidated layer, transitioning to stronger material below. If the penetrator encounters variable-density material — a buried rock, an unusually soft zone — it may stop short or deviate from vertical.

The structural loads during impact are high, placing demands on all internal components. The working area of 8 to 12 metres limits robot mobility for subsequent construction.

---

## Primary Risk

**Uneven penetration in variable-density regolith.**  
The penetration depth and verticality depend on regolith properties at the specific touchdown point. Current orbital data does not provide sufficient resolution to predict these properties with confidence. Phase 0 Demonstrator 1 must characterise the range of acceptable regolith conditions.

---

## Best Suited For

Missions where fast rigid fixation is the priority and the smaller working area is acceptable. Also the most conservative choice if site selection cannot guarantee a large flat zone.

---

## Open Questions for Engineers

1. What is the maximum slope angle at which the penetrator achieves acceptable verticality?
2. What is the pull-out force of the fluke system under the maximum expected mast loading?
3. Does the impact load during touchdown exceed the structural limits of any pod component?
4. What is the minimum acceptable penetration depth for long-term mast stability?
