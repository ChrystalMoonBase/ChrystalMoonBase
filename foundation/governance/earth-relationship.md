## Chrystal Moon Base — Governance

## Legal Foundation

CMB is founded on the Outer Space Treaty (1967), specifically Article II: outer space including the Moon is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by use, or by any other means. No nation owns the Moon. CMB does not claim any part of it.

CMB interprets this as: the Moon belongs to all of humanity. What is built there should serve all of humanity. Therefore it is built openly, governed neutrally, and accessible to all qualified partners.

---

## Relationship with Space Agencies

CMB seeks partnerships with space agencies, not subordination to them. The relationship is:

- CMB provides the open-source concept and the founding infrastructure
- Space agencies provide technical expertise, launch capability, institutional credibility, and resources
- Both parties benefit from the collaboration
- Neither party controls the other

CMB does not accept conditions that would make it the exclusive project of any single nation or agency. An ESA partnership does not give ESA exclusive control over the base. A NASA partnership does not give NASA exclusive control. Both partners benefit alongside all other partners.

---

## Relationship with Regulatory Authorities

Launches require approval from national launch authorities. In practice, this means CMB must work with the regulatory framework of whatever nation's territory or vehicle is used for launch.

CMB will comply fully with all applicable space law and launch regulations. It will not attempt to circumvent regulatory requirements.

If regulatory requirements conflict with the open-science principle — for example, if a national authority requires technology export control that would prevent open publication — CMB will seek alternative launch providers rather than compromise the open-science commitment.

---

## Political Neutrality

CMB flies no national flags. It celebrates no national holidays. It plays no national anthems.

This is not an anti-national position. It is a recognition that a base serving all of humanity cannot be seen to favour any nation — and perception matters as much as intent in international affairs.

Residents bring their own cultural identities and practices to the base. Cultural diversity is welcomed. National flags on personal items in personal spaces are fine. National flags on shared infrastructure are not.
